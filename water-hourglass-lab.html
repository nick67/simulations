Why does the flow slow down over time?
Watch the flow-rate graph: it starts at its highest value and curves downward the whole way, unlike a granular hourglass where the rate stays flat almost to the end. That's because liquid flow through an orifice is driven by pressure, and the pressure at the hole depends on how tall the column of water sitting above it is — its head height. This is Torricelli's law: the exit speed of the water is v = √(2·g·h), where h is the head height. As the top bulb drains, h shrinks, so the exit speed — and therefore the flow rate — shrinks with it. A water clock is consequently a much less reliable timer than a sand one, which is part of why sand hourglasses won out historically for measuring fixed intervals.
Where does √(2gh) come from?
It falls straight out of energy conservation. A parcel of water at the surface, height h above the hole, has gravitational potential energy m·g·h. By the time it reaches the orifice it has converted essentially all of that into kinetic energy, ½·m·v². Setting them equal and solving for v gives v = √(2gh) — the same speed an object would reach falling from rest through height h. The actual flow rate is then Q = Cd·A·√(2gh), where A is the orifice area and Cd is a discharge coefficient (around 0.6 here) that accounts for the jet narrowing slightly as it exits — a real effect called the vena contracta.
Why hole size matters differently than for sand
For this liquid model, flow rate scales directly with orifice area — double the hole's radius and you roughly quadruple the flow (A ∝ d²). That's a much gentler relationship than dry granular flow, where the empirical Beverloo correlation gives something closer to Q ∝ (D − k·d)^2.5 — a steeper, more sensitive dependence caused by grains jamming and arching near the edges of the opening in a way a continuous fluid simply doesn't.
About this simulation
The vessel's volume-vs-height relationship is computed by numerically integrating its true tapered cross-section (treating the glass as a solid of revolution), so the head height used in Torricelli's law comes from the vessel's actual shape rather than a flat-tank approximation. Each frame, the simulation solves dV/dt = −Cd·A·√(2gh) directly rather than playing back a precomputed curve, so changing gravity or hole size mid-run produces a genuinely different trajectory. The estimated total drain time is likewise found by numerically fast-forwarding the same equation, not by a constant-rate guess — which is why it updates as the flow itself decelerates.
