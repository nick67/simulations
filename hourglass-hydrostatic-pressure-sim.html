Hourglass Lab

A sand hourglass empties at a constant rate. A water hourglass doesn't — because the outflow speed depends on the height of water above the hole. That's Torricelli's law: v = √(2gh), a direct consequence of hydrostatic pressure increasing with depth. Compare the two below.

Tip: switch one panel to water and one to sand, run both, and watch the flow-rate graphs.

Why this happens

In a granular material like sand, friction between grains forms a stable "arch" right at the opening. The arch sets the flow rate, and it doesn't care how tall the sand pile above it is — so the rate stays essentially constant until the chamber is empty. In a liquid there's no arch: pressure at the opening is hydrostatic, p = ρgh, and it pushes harder when the column is taller. Applying Bernoulli's equation between the free surface and the opening gives the efflux speed v = √(2gh) — Torricelli's law. As the water level h drops, the flow slows down, which is why a simple water hourglass (without a specially shaped, compensating bulb) is a poor clock: equal volumes do not drain in equal times.
