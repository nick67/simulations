Why does the flow rate stay (almost) constant?
Watch the flow-rate graph: after a brief settling moment it flattens out and stays nearly flat for most of the run — even though the top bulb is steadily losing height. That's the opposite of how a liquid behaves: water drains faster when the column above the hole is taller, because the pressure at the hole scales with height. Sand doesn't, because grains push sideways against each other and the container walls, forming brief arches and force chains that carry weight outward into the glass rather than straight down. Past a certain depth, adding more sand on top barely increases the pressure at the neck. This is sometimes called the Janssen effect, and it's the reason hourglasses are reliable timers — the flow rate barely depends on how full the bulb still is, right up until the very end, when the supply runs out and the rate visibly drops off.
Why does neck width matter so much?
Try halving the neck width slider and watch the flow rate — it drops by far more than half. For an idealized fluid you'd expect the flow through an opening to scale with its area, i.e. with width². Real granular flow through an orifice follows something closer to the empirical Beverloo correlation, Q ∝ (D − k·d)^2.5, where D is the opening diameter and d is the grain diameter. The exponent of 2.5 (steeper than 2) comes from an "empty annulus" right at the edge of the opening — grains can't use the outermost sliver of the hole because they can't squeeze through a gap smaller than their own size. That's also why the grain-size slider has a real effect here: coarser grains eat more of the neck's effective width before any of them can even start falling through.
About this simulation
Each grain is a cell in a grid that follows simple falling-sand rules: drop straight down if the cell below is empty, otherwise slip diagonally if a diagonal cell is open, otherwise stay put. This is a cellular automaton, not a rigid-body or molecular dynamics simulation — it's a deliberately lightweight model that still reproduces three things real sand does: a roughly constant drain rate, a settling pile with a stable slope (an approximation of the real angle of repose), and a funnel-shaped depression forming on the top surface as it drains. Flow rate and grain counts in the readouts above are measured directly from grains crossing the neck — they aren't pre-scripted.
